177 — what geometry can an inline formula be given?
==============================================================================
ANSWER: A PARENT-LINE BOX, AND NOTHING NARROWER. NO FORMULA-SPECIFIC
GEOMETRY EXISTS, AND NONE CAN BE DERIVED FROM ANY STORED FIELD.

That closes the question rather than leaving it open, which is what was asked.

WHAT IS AVAILABLE, AND FROM WHICH FIELD
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Measured over 150 documents, 108,594 Formula objects:

   Formula.props.region                                    0     (0.0%)
   a `mathpix_lines` surface realization anchor      107,222    (98.7%)
   that anchor's line payload has a `region`         107,222    (98.7%)
   the realization carries `offset` and `length`     107,222    (98.7%)

So the field is:

   doc.stream("mathpix_lines").payload[ realization.start ]["region"]

reached from the Formula's own surface realization. It is a MathPix
page-image pixel box, in the same coordinate space as an Equation's region,
and it needs the same per-page scaling to reach a raster (out/170: MathPix
pages are 250 dpi, `rasterize` floors at 400, and 11 of 305 documents carry
more than one page_width).

Where geometry fusion has run there is also `_geom.x0_norm / x1_norm / y_norm`
on the same line — normalised PDF-space coordinates for the LINE. Coverage is
poor and quality is worse: present on 14,120 of 108,594 (13.0%), of which
3,413 are `fallback`, and the match similarity has a median of 0.646. It is a
second reading of the same line box, not a finer one.

WHY THE LINE BOX IS NOT ENOUGH — THE NUMBER THAT DECIDES THIS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
   formulas per parent line:  1 -> 18,571 lines
                              2 ->  6,506
                              3 ->  1,791
                              4 ->    557
                              5+ ->   1,103

   formulas SHARING a line with at least one other:  88,651  (82.7%)

Four inline formulas in five sit on a line with another one. Giving each of
them the line's box does not locate them — it gives several different objects
the same rectangle, which is not geometry, it is a claim that they are in the
same place. For the 17.3% that are alone on their line, the box is real and
correct, and that distinction is worth carrying rather than averaging away.

WHY IT CANNOT BE NARROWED
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The realization already knows WHERE in the line the formula sits — `offset`
and `length` are a character span into the line's text, present on all 98.7%.
Turning a character offset into an x-range needs per-character advance widths,
and nothing stores them:

  * MathPix text lines carry exactly these keys, and no word- or
    character-level geometry among them:
      cnt, column, confidence, confidence_rate, conversion_output, font_size,
      id, is_handwritten, is_printed, line, parent_id, region, subtype, text,
      text_display, type

  * `cnt` is not a rescue. On text lines it is 4 points in 20,774 of 20,774
    cases — an axis-aligned box, the same information as `region`.

  * `*.lines.pdfminer-chars.bak.json` is named for characters but stores LINE
    regions: keys id, region, text, text_display, type. No chars.

  * `_geom` carries `x0_norm, x1_norm, y_norm, indent_norm, sim, fallback,
    pdf_text` — all line-level.

Proportional interpolation across the line is not an option and should not be
offered as one. Text is not monospaced, and inline mathematics is set in a
different family with different metrics from the prose around it, so the
character offset is least proportional exactly where the formula is. It would
produce a plausible box that is wrong by an unknown amount — the failure this
corpus keeps producing, manufactured deliberately.

WHAT WOULD ACTUALLY PRODUCE IT, AND WHAT IT WOULD COST
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Per-character boxes DO exist upstream, briefly. `chars_to_lines.py` consumes
pdfplumber chars with x0/y0/x1/y1 and aggregates them into lines; the chars
are discarded and never persisted. Recovering formula-level geometry would
mean keeping them and aligning the MathPix line text to the PDF text layer
character by character.

Two things make that harder than it sounds, and both are measured:

  1. It is born-digital only. A scanned document has no text layer, and the
     scanned books are where the inline-formula counts are largest.
  2. The two texts do not align. `_geom` median similarity is 0.646, and
     out/162 established that `_geom.pdf_text` is not merely lossy but
     WORD-SCRAMBLED on affected lines, with C0 control characters where the
     /ToUnicode map failed. Character-level alignment against that is not a
     lookup, it is a research problem with a wrong answer available at every
     step.

An alternative exists and is honest about its cost: render the line, measure
ink components with inkdrill, and assign the run of components covering the
formula's character span. That is a real measurement rather than an
interpolation, but it needs the same alignment to know WHICH components, so it
moves the problem rather than solving it.

CONCLUSION
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
An inline formula can be given its parent line's box, from
`mathpix_lines.payload[anchor].region`, for 98.7% of them. That box is
formula-specific for 17.3% and shared for 82.7%.

Nothing in any stored field can narrow it further. The question is closed:
there is no field to read, and the only routes to a real answer require
re-extraction plus an alignment that the measured similarity says is not
reliable.

If a consumer needs inline-formula geometry, the correct move is to record
what is true — the line box, flagged as shared where it is shared — rather
than to synthesise a per-formula rectangle. A shared box labelled shared is
usable. A synthesised box is indistinguishable from a measured one, and this
is the third task in a row where that distinction was the whole finding.
